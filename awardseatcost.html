Award seat cost

What does an award seat really cost the airline? Almost nothing on an empty flight, and a paying passenger's fare on a full one.

When a member books a seat with points, the airline carries one more passenger and, if the flight would have filled, loses the sale of that seat. So the cost of an award seat is the larger of two numbers: the marginal cost of carrying one more person (fuel, catering, fees) and the displacement cost, the revenue the seat would otherwise have earned. That second number is the bid price of the last seat, the same opportunity cost that revenue management uses to accept or reject any booking. Compare it with what the loyalty programme pays the airline for the seat, and you know when releasing award seats helps both sides.

The flight, fares, demand, costs and programme payment are illustrative assumptions, not any airline's or programme's figures.

4. Why saver awards disappear on peak dates

Members read an empty award calendar at Christmas as the programme hiding seats. From the airline's side it is the cost curve at work, for three reasons that stack up:

  1. The flight is expected to fill. Past about 90% expected demand, an award seat is very likely to take the place of a paying passenger, so its cost jumps from the marginal cost to a fare.
  2. The fares it displaces are higher. Peak dates carry peak fares and more late, high-fare bookings, so the seat the award takes is worth more.
  3. The forecast is more certain. Holiday demand is predictable, so revenue management is confident the seat will sell and has little reason to take a chance on it.

A saver award pays the airline the same amount on every date, so on peak dates its line sits under the cost curve and no saver seats open. That is why programmes add a second, higher price: a standard award, or dynamic award pricing tied to the cash fare, which pays enough to cover the displacement. Saver seats can also reappear close to departure: if bookings come in below the forecast, the bid price falls and the RM system releases more award seats.

More on this trade-off: Loyalty 101, chapter 10: Where Loyalty Meets RM, and on what members get for their points: the award value comparator.

How it works

The flight and its demand

An invented 3½-hour domestic flight with 150 economy seats and five fare classes. Each example flight has its own fare level (85%, 100% and 130% of a base fare ladder of CA$620, 450, 340, 250 and 180), class mix and demand variability. Expected demand in class j is μj = L · 150 · sj, where L is the expected demand as a share of seats and sj the class share. Demand is a negative binomial draw (a Poisson whose mean has a gamma-distributed error with coefficient of variation cv), so its variance is μ + (cv · μ)². The inputs are in assets/data/awardseatcost.json.

Displacement cost: the bid price of the last seat

Let V(x) be the expected revenue from paying passengers with x seats, when classes book from the lowest fare to the highest and the airline protects seats for higher fares optimally (the static single-leg model of Brumelle and McGill, 1993, solved by dynamic programming as in Talluri and van Ryzin, 2004, section 2.2). For class j with fare fj, the seats protected for the higher classes are the largest y with Vj−1(y) − Vj−1(y − 1) > fj, and

Vj(x) = E[ fj · min(Dj, x − y) + Vj−1(x − min(Dj, x − y)) ].

The k-th award seat leaves 150 − k seats for sale, so its displacement cost is Δk = V(150 − k + 1) − V(150 − k). For the first award seat this is the expected marginal seat revenue of the last seat, which EMSR-b (Belobaba, 1989) approximates and Littlewood's rule (1972) gives for two classes.

Cost, release and net value

  • Marginal cost m = extra fuel + catering + fees. The defaults assume about 100 kg for a passenger and bag, 3–4% of that extra weight burned as fuel per flight hour (a common rule of thumb, not a measured figure), and a jet fuel price near US$185 a barrel.
  • Cost of the k-th award seat: ck = max(m, Δk). Δk never falls as k grows, so neither does ck.
  • Release by cost: open award seats while ck < p, the programme's payment per seat. Booked awards = min(seats released, members asking). Airline net = Σ (p − ck) over the booked seats. The fixed allocation uses the same formula with its own number of seats released.
  • The cost curve in section 1 repeats the calculation of c1 for every L from 50% to 110%, holding the fares, class mix and variability of the chosen flight. The break-even is where it crosses p, interpolated between whole percentages.

Limitations

  • The maximum of the two costs is a simplification. Strictly, the expected cost is m · P(seat would fly empty) + Δk: if the seat would have sold, the airline carries a passenger either way. The two differ by less than m, and they agree at both ends of the curve.
  • Demand in each class is independent of what is open: nobody buys up when a cheap class closes (RM 101, chapter 05). Once the flight is expected to be full, the curve levels off near the lowest fare still on sale; with buy-up it would keep rising.
  • Award seats are decided once, at the opening of sales. Real systems re-optimise as bookings come in, and a connecting award displaces seats on several flights at once (network bid prices).
  • The programme's payment is one fixed number per seat. Real arrangements vary: some pay by cabin, route or date, and dynamic award pricing ties the payment to the cash fare. The value of availability for member engagement, which is why programmes accept some awards that don't pay their way, isn't modelled.
  • Taxes and carrier charges the award passenger pays, cancellations, no-shows and overbooking are left out.
