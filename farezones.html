Redrawing the fare zones

Four fare zones, one river crossing, and a $1.25 to $3.25 jump between neighbours. What would other maps cost?

The ARTM prices an all-modes trip by the outermost zone it touches: A is the island of Montréal, B is Laval and the Longueuil agglomeration, C the suburbs, D the towns beyond. Two people living a few hundred metres apart on either side of a zone line pay very different fares for nearly the same trip. Below, the current zones are compared with three alternatives, each re-optimized for revenue, ridership and two fairness limits you set: concentric rings around downtown, a fare per kilometre and a single flat fare.

Figures are illustrative. Fares and zones are ARTM's published ones (in force 1 July 2026); the trips, travel times and price response come from a model built on census data and the ARTM 2023 survey's published totals, not from ticket sales. Revenue and ridership are indices, today = 100. Details under "How it works".

The revenue management parallel

A fare structure is a pricing grid, and the vocabulary carries over almost word for word from airline revenue management.

Transit faresAirline pricing
Zones A, AB, ABC, ABCDFare classes: a short ladder of price points, each valid for a set of trips
Paying for the outermost zone touchedPricing a journey by its longest or most expensive leg, rather than as an origin–destination (O&D) product
Per-kilometre fareMileage-based fares, the pre-deregulation norm, smooth but blind to demand
Flat fareOne price for every O&D: simple, and cross-subsidizing short trips with long ones or the reverse
A zone boundaryA fare fence: a rule that decides who may buy which price
Driving to Henri-Bourassa instead of MontmorencyBuy-down: a customer stepping over the fence to a cheaper fare

A good fence separates customers whose willingness to pay really differs, such as a Saturday-night stay separating leisure and business travellers. A zone line through a dense neighbourhood is a weak fence: the people on each side are alike, so it mostly creates resentment and buy-down (the park-and-ride one station further in). That is why the fairness limits here are framed as a maximum jump between neighbours: they cap how much a fence can be worth to step over. Fences and branded fares are in RM 101, chapter 01, and the fare family designer shows the same trade-off from the airline side: a ladder with bigger steps earns more per sale but invites more buy-down.

How it works

The model behind the page is scripts/mtl/farezones/build_farezones.py. It runs once and stores every optimized solution in farezones.json; the page only looks them up and computes the fare of your trip with the same rules.

Geography and today's fares

The region is cut into 636 cells: each is the part of one H3 hexagon (resolution 7, about 5 km²) inside one municipality, carrying its 2021 census population and jobs. The 84 municipalities and their zones come from ARTM's list of municipalities by zone; ARTM doesn't publish the zone boundaries as GIS data, so the zone shapes are the census subdivision (municipality) boundaries grouped by that list. Single regular fares from ARTM's grid in force on 1 July 2026 (consulted 1 October 2026):

A trip that uses the metro, REM or a train pays the all-modes fare of the outermost zone it touches. A trip whose quickest path is bus-only pays the $3.75 bus fare, my reading of ARTM's grid (bus titles at $3.75 are valid on buses in zones A to C). Every structure on the page redesigns the all-modes fare only; bus-only trips pay $3.75 or the all-modes fare, whichever is lower.

Travel times (a proxy)

There is no timetable routing yet. Transit time is the quicker of two options. The first is a bus-only trip: 10 minutes of waiting and walking, then the distance at 17 km/h. The second uses rapid transit through one of the k = 5 stations nearest each end. Getting to the station means walking up to 1.2 km at 4.8 km/h, a feeder bus at 18 km/h plus 10 minutes, or park-and-ride beyond 12 km from downtown. The ride itself is at 32 km/h on the metro, 38 km/h on the REM and 45 km/h on exo trains, with waits of 3, 4 and 15 minutes and a 4-minute penalty to change mode. Car time is the road distance (1.3 × the straight line) at 22–50 km/h depending on trip length, plus 6 minutes for a downtown destination. Distances are straight lines times a detour factor, so a river with few bridges counts no more than a street.

Stations: STM metro from STM's GTFS feed (2026 summer schedule), REM stations in service from OpenStreetMap. exo commuter-rail stations are approximate: exo's GTFS couldn't be downloaded when the page was built, so each municipality served by exo lines 1 to 5 gets one station at its population-weighted centre. Demand

Trips between cells follow a doubly constrained gravity model:

Tij = ai bj Oi Dj exp(−β dij),

where Oi is population, Dj is jobs + 0.35 × population (non-work trips follow where people live), dij is the straight-line distance, β = 0.16 per km, and ai, bj make every row and column add up. The total is scaled to the ARTM 2023 survey: 8.9 million weekday trips, 66% by car and 13% by transit, for 4.6 million people surveyed. That gives car or transit trips a day for the 4.19 million people modelled, with a mean straight-line length of km. The matrix itself is illustrative; only the totals are anchored to official figures.

Price response

Each trip chooses transit or car with a binary logit:

Pij(transit) = 1 / (1 + exp(−[αr(i) + βt(tTij − tCij) + βf(fij − cij)])),

with βt = −0.035 per minute. cij is the car's cost: $0.20 per road km plus parking of $8 within 1.5 km of downtown and $3 within 4 km. The fare coefficient βf is set so the average point elasticity βf f (1 − P) equals the slider value. The constants αr, one per home region (Montréal, Laval, Longueuil, north and south suburbs), are set so that transit carries 13 / (66 + 13) = 16.5% of car and transit trips, split across regions like the survey's morning-peak transit trips by home region (236,000; 33,000; 37,000; 20,000; 23,000). Longer, pricier trips with a good car alternative respond more, which is how the price response varies with distance.

The elasticity range brackets Paulley et al. (2006): about −0.3 in the short run for metro, −0.4 for bus and −0.6 for suburban rail, rising to −0.6 for metro and −1.0 for bus in the long run. With βt fixed, each elasticity implies a value of time:

Optimization

For each fare structure s, with parameters θ, the model maximizes

(1 − w) · R(θ) / R0 + w · Q(θ) / Q0

where R = Σ Tij Pij fij is revenue, Q = Σ Tij Pij is ridership, and the 0 subscripts are today's values. It does so subject to three limits:

  • revenue floor: R / R0 ≥ 0.95;
  • jump limit: for every pair of neighbouring cells and every destination, |fij − fi′j| ≤ J (the "largest fare jump" setting);
  • who pays more: the share of today's riders whose fare rises is at most S.

Rings: three radii from 4 to 40 km in 2 km steps (969 combinations) and four prices in $0.25 steps, the inner one up to $5.00, each step outwards between $0 and $2.50. A trip pays the price of the outermost ring it touches, like today. "Snapped" puts each municipality wholly in the ring of its population-weighted centre, except Montréal, Laval and Longueuil, which span several rings and are cut along hexagons. Per km: a base fare of $1.50–4.50, $0.00–0.40 per km, a cap of $4–12, and the distance rounded to the half kilometre. Flat: one price from $2 to $12. Every combination is evaluated exactly (riders respond through the logit), for 5 elasticities × 5 weights × 3 jump limits × 3 shares = 225 settings. When no option meets all three limits, the page shows the closest one: the fewest riders paying more among options that keep the revenue floor and the jump limit. It's flagged "limit not met".

What the numbers measure

  • Revenue and ridership index: model revenue and transit trips, today = 100.
  • Pay more / pay less: share of today's riders (model trips, before anyone reacts) whose fare goes up or down.
  • Largest jump: the biggest fare difference between two neighbouring cells for the same destination.
  • Cliff exposure: share of today's riders who would save $1 or more on their trip by starting from a neighbouring cell. Both cliff measures use the all-modes fare.
  • Winners and losers: the average change in fare per trip for today's riders starting in each area. Montréal, Laval and Longueuil are split by H3 resolution-6 hexagons and named by distance and direction from downtown.

Limitations

  • No ticket sales, validations or origin–destination data. ARTM's survey microdata are available only on request, so the trip matrix comes from a gravity model. No figure here is ARTM's or any operator's revenue.
  • Only the single regular fare. Monthly passes (most commuters' ticket), 10-trip books and reduced fares (ages 6–17, students, 65+, free for children under 12 and, in some towns, seniors) are left out, and each trip responds to the single-trip price.
  • Jobs come from the 2021 census, collected in May 2021 during the pandemic. They count only workers with a usual place of work, so downtown is likely under-weighted. Outside the Montréal metropolitan area (most of zone D), jobs are set at 0.4 × population.
  • Travel times are a proxy, exo stations are approximate, and each trip's path (bus-only or rail) is fixed by time and doesn't switch when prices change. A rider can't move to another station to dodge a cliff, which is the buy-down the cliff exposure measure only counts.
  • Car or transit only: no walking or cycling, no trips that disappear or appear with price, no crowding and no capacity limits. Revenue sharing between STM, exo, RTL, STL and the REM is out of scope.
  • The bus-only $3.75 rule is my reading of ARTM's grid and is kept unchanged in every structure.
