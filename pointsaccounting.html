Accounting for points

Who gets paid, when the airline earns it, and how three accounting methods differ.

We follow one ticket: a member pays and earns points, flies, then uses the points for two awards. First the idea of deferred revenue, then the three ways airlines have booked it, side by side, then where the profit comes from.

Illustrative numbers: a stylised ticket, not the figures or accounting policies of any airline.

Change the ticket

Deferred revenue: cash now, revenue later

Today's rule, IFRS 15

The member pays the airline on the day of the booking, but at that point the airline hasn't done anything yet. Money received for something not yet delivered is not revenue: it is deferred revenue, a debt to the member that the airline pays back in kind, with a flight and later with awards. It becomes revenue only when each promise is kept. Points work exactly like the ticket, except the wait is longer, and some points are never used at all (breakage).

Who receives which money, and when

Cash changes hands only twice. Revenue is earned in four pieces.

If a bank buys the points to give to its cardholders, the money comes from the bank instead, on the day it buys them, while the member gets the points for free. The airline still owes the award, so the points' share of the bank's payment is deferred in exactly the same way.

Three methods, side by side

Same ticket, same cash, different timing

IFRS 15 is the rule today. Before it, airlines either valued the points first and gave the flight what was left (the residual method), or didn't treat the points as something sold at all and only set aside what the awards would cost (incremental cost). Step through the ticket's life and compare.

Every step at a glance

Where the profit comes from

Collect at value, deliver at cost

The points' share is booked at what points are worth to members, but the airline pays it back in seats, which often cost it little. Four things turn that gap into profit.

The spreadAwards mostly go on seats that would have flown empty. Flying one costs fees, fuel and catering, far less than the value deferred for it.
BreakagePoints never used were paid for and cost nothing to honour. Their value is released as revenue alongside the awards.
Partner salesBanks pay for the points and for the airline's brand and members. That marketing share is revenue right away.
The floatCash comes in years before the awards go out: free funding. United, Delta and American borrowed billions against their programmes in 2020–21.

Six words to know

The vocabulary of a loyalty note
Deferred revenueCash received for something not delivered yet. IFRS 15 calls it a contract liability. It becomes revenue when the promise is kept.
LiabilitySomething the company owes. Unflown tickets and unused points are both liabilities: they are owed in seats, not cash.
Standalone selling priceWhat each promise would sell for on its own. IFRS 15 splits a fare between flight and points in proportion to these values.
BreakagePoints that will never be used. Their value is released little by little with each award, not when the points expire.
ProvisionMoney set aside for an expected cost. Under the incremental cost method, the points are only a provision for the cost of the awards.
DevaluationRaising the points needed for an award. Points already issued keep the value they were deferred at, but cost less to honour.

How it works

The ticket is sold in month 0 and flown in month 2. The member uses 60% of the points expected to be used in month 10, the rest in month 28, and the unused points expire in month 40. Defaults follow Loyalty 101, chapter 04: $500, 5,000 points, 1.3¢ a point, 15% never used.

Points expected to be used E = N × (1 − b) Value of the points V = N × v × (1 − b) IFRS 15 points D = P × V / (P + V) flight = P − D Residual points D = V (at most P) flight = P − D Incremental cost flight = P provision = E × cost per point, expensed at the flight Each award of R points releases D × R / E (its own value plus its share of breakage)

The lab looks at 1,000 points over their whole life. Raising award prices by x divides the value of a point and the cost of an award by 1 + x and adds 1.5 points of breakage per 10%. An award on an empty seat costs 0.15¢ a point; one that displaces a paying passenger costs what that seat would have sold for. The fare and the partner price don't change, so whatever is no longer deferred for flight points is passenger revenue on the day of the flight.

The rules over time. Before 2008, many IFRS companies treated points as a marketing cost. IFRIC 13 (issued 2007, applied from 2008) required part of the sale to be deferred for the points, using either relative values or the residual method. IFRS 15 and its US twin ASC 606 replaced everything in 2018 with relative standalone selling prices. Until then, US airlines generally used incremental cost for miles earned on flights. When breakage can't be estimated, IFRS 15 holds the unused points' share until they expire instead; most large programmes do estimate it.

Limitations

  • One ticket, a fixed redemption schedule, and breakage that turns out exactly as expected. The Breakage & liability model follows a whole programme month by month, including revised estimates.
  • The cost of the flight itself, taxes, interest and currency are left out; profit is the profit on the points only.
  • Real devaluations also change how members fly and spend, and banks reprice their contracts at renewal. The lab ignores both.
