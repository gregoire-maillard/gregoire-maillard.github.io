Accounting for points

Three ways to book a loyalty point, and IFRS 15 step by step.

A member who flies and earns points has bought two things at once: a seat today and an award later. How much of the fare belongs to the points, when it becomes revenue, and what sits on the balance sheet in between depends on the accounting method. This page follows one ticket through the books, entry by entry, then compares IFRS 15 with the methods it replaced.

Illustrative numbers: a stylised ticket and programme, not the figures or accounting policies of any airline.

1The fare is splitThe flight and the points are two separate promises. The fare is shared between them in proportion to what each would sell for on its own.
2The points wait as a liabilityTheir share is deferred revenue: cash received for something not delivered yet. It sits on the balance sheet next to the tickets sold but not yet flown.
3Revenue follows the awardsEach award releases its share of the liability into revenue, including a slice for the points expected never to be used (breakage).

Points sold to a partner

Co-brand cards, hotels, car rental, retail

Many points are never earned in the air: banks and other partners buy them to reward their own customers. What a bank pays buys more than points: the airline's brand on the card, access to its members, advertising. Those are separate promises too, so the payment is split the same way a fare is. Only the points' share is deferred; the marketing share is revenue as those services are delivered.

The vocabulary

The words in an airline's loyalty note
LiabilitySomething the company owes: cash, a service or, here, a seat. Points are a liability because members can claim flights with them.
Deferred revenueCash received for something not delivered yet. IFRS 15 calls it a contract liability. It turns into revenue when the promise is kept.
Advance ticket salesThe deferred revenue for tickets sold but not yet flown, also called the air traffic liability. Usually an airline's largest current liability.
Performance obligationA distinct promise in a contract. A ticket that earns points contains two: the flight, and the right to a future award.
Standalone selling priceWhat a promise would sell for on its own. For a point, roughly what it's worth when redeemed, reduced for the chance it is never used.
AllocationSharing one price among the promises in a contract, in proportion to their standalone selling prices.
BreakagePoints that will never be redeemed. When it can be estimated, its value is recognised in step with redemptions, not when points expire.
Change in estimateWhen expected breakage is revised, the balance is restated as if the new rate had always applied and the difference goes through revenue at once (the catch-up).
Current and non-currentThe part of the liability expected to be redeemed within twelve months is current; the rest is non-current.
ProvisionA liability of uncertain amount, measured at the expected cost of settling it. Under the incremental cost method, the points are a provision for the cost of the awards.
Principal or agentWhen a member redeems on a partner airline, the programme only arranges the flight, so it books revenue net of what it pays the partner.
Financing componentWhen cash arrives long before delivery, as with a multi-year prepayment from a card issuer, IFRS 15 may require interest to accrue on the liability.

How it works

The ticket

A member pays P for a ticket and earns N points, each worth v to the member. A share b of points is expected never to be used. The ticket is sold in month 0 and flown in month 2. The member redeems 60% of the points expected to be used in month 10 and the rest in month 28; the remaining points lapse in month 40. Each award costs the airline c per point redeemed to fly. The default ticket is the one in Loyalty 101, chapter 04: $500, 5,000 points, 1.3¢ a point and 15% breakage.

Points expected to be redeemed E = N × (1 − b) breakage points B = N − E Standalone value of the points SSP = N × v × (1 − b) IFRS 15 (relative) deferred D = P × SSP / (P + SSP) flight F = P − D Residual D = SSP (at most P) F = P − D Incremental cost F = P provision = E × c, expensed when the flight is flown

Under the two deferral methods, the points' share is released as awards are delivered:

Breakage with redemptions an award of R points releases D × R / E of which the award itself D × R / N, breakage D × R × (1/E − 1/N) Breakage when points lapse an award releases D × R / N; the last D × B / N when the points lapse

Under incremental cost, flying an award uses up the provision and doesn't touch revenue. Under the deferral methods, the cost of flying the award is an expense when the award is flown. The balance sheet leaves out the cost of the original flight and everything else in the airline, so equity is just the profit on this ticket so far.

The rules over time

Before 2008, IFRS had no specific rule and many companies treated points as a marketing cost, accruing only what the awards were expected to cost. IFRIC 13 Customer Loyalty Programmes, issued in 2007 and applied from 2008, required part of the sale to be deferred as revenue for the award credits at their fair value, without saying how to split the price, so both the relative and the residual approaches were used. IFRS 15 replaced it in 2018 and requires relative standalone selling prices; its guidance on customer options and unexercised rights (paragraphs B39–B47) is where breakage comes from. In the United States, ASC 606 took effect the same year with the same model. Before it, US airlines generally used incremental cost for miles earned on flights and multiple-element accounting for miles sold to partners.

Limitations

  • One ticket and a fixed redemption schedule. A real programme holds millions of balances at different ages; the Breakage & liability model follows them month by month.
  • Breakage turns out exactly as expected here. When it doesn't, the estimate is revised and a catch-up adjustment goes through revenue, which this page doesn't show.
  • The value per point is an input. In practice estimating it is a judgement of its own, often based on what similar awards would cost to buy.
  • No financing component, taxes, currency, or partner redemptions (where the programme acts as an agent and books revenue net).
  • The partner example splits a payment into two parts only; real co-brand agreements have more elements and are recognised over several years.
