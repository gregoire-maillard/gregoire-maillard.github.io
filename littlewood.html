Littlewood's rule

One flight, two fares. Cheap tickets sell first. How many seats do you keep back for the passengers who pay more?

Keep too few and late business travellers find the flight full. Keep too many and seats fly empty that a leisure passenger would have bought. In 1972 Ken Littlewood, at BOAC, gave the answer that all airline seat control still builds on: keep protecting one more seat as long as the chance that high-fare demand reaches it is greater than the low fare divided by the high fare. Move the sliders and watch the answer change. Full background in Revenue Management 101, chapter 03. Open the full page ↗

Fares and demand on this page are illustrative assumptions, not any airline's data.

The flight

The two fares

High-fare demand forecast

High-fare demand is uncertain: about two flights in three land within one spread of the average. Low-fare customers are assumed to be plentiful: there are always enough of them to buy every seat they're offered, and they all book before the high-fare customers.

The rule, one seat at a time

Each bar is one more seat you could keep for the high fare. Its height is the chance that high-fare demand gets that far, so that a high-fare passenger is there to buy it. Keeping the seat is worth the high fare times that chance; selling it now is worth the low fare for certain. The two are equal where the bars cross the dashed line at low fare ÷ high fare.

Fill the flight

Same customers, three choices

One departure, booked three ways. Low-fare customers book first until the seats on sale to them are gone. Then the high-fare customers arrive, and only the protected seats are left for them. Protect too few and some are turned away; protect too many and seats fly empty.

Too few, too many, just right

Average revenue per flight

One flight can go either way: on a quiet day protecting fewer seats would have earned more, on a busy day more. What Littlewood's level wins is the average, over every departure with this forecast. The curve shows that average for every possible protection level.

From two fares to many

EMSR-b, the industry workhorse

Real flights sell six to twenty booking classes, not two. The same comparison is made at every step down the fare ladder: how many seats to keep for all the classes above against the next class down. EMSR-b (Belobaba, 1992) merges the classes above into one, with their average fare and combined demand, and applies Littlewood's rule to it. These are the protection levels it sets on the example flight in the booking-limits simulator: 150 seats, six classes from $620 down to $130.

How it works

The model

One flight leg with C seats and two fares, fH > fL. Low-fare customers book first and are plentiful. High-fare demand DH is uncertain: a normal draw with average μ and spread σ, rounded down to whole passengers (a negative draw counts as zero). Protecting y seats means the low fare is sold until C − y seats are gone (its booking limit), and the last y are kept for the high fare.

Revenue. With y seats protected, average revenue is R(y) = fL (C − y) + fH E[min(DH, y)], and for whole-number demand E[min(DH, y)] = Σk=1…y P(DH ≥ k).

Littlewood's rule. Protecting the y-th seat changes revenue by R(y) − R(y − 1) = fH P(DH ≥ y) − fL. That gain shrinks as y grows, so the best protection level y* is the largest y with P(DH ≥ y) ≥ fL / fH, capped at C. Because demand is a normal draw rounded down, P(DH ≥ y) = 1 − Φ((y − μ) / σ) exactly for whole y ≥ 1, which is what the bars show, and y* is the whole part of μ + σ · Φ−1(1 − fL / fH). With the default numbers, the ones in RM 101's worked example: 40 + 10 × 0.253 = 42.5, so protect 42 seats.

Fill the flight. "Too few" and "too many" protect one spread (σ, rounded, at least one seat) below and above y*. Each flight draws one high-fare demand from the forecast; all three cabins see the same customers. "New flight" draws another. The random generator is seeded, so the page always opens on the same flight. Which seat a passenger takes is only for the picture: airlines protect a number of seats, not particular seats.

From two fares to many. EMSR-b protection for classes 1 to j against class j+1 uses the composite fare f̄ = Σ fiμi / Σ μi and demand N(Σ μi, Σ σi2): yj = μ̄ + σ̄ · Φ−1(1 − fj+1 / f̄), rounded to the nearest seat as in the booking-limits simulator, whose default flight it uses. Rounding to the nearest seat rather than down can give one seat more than the two-fare rule above.

Limitations

  • Low fares all book before high fares. When they arrive mixed, a fixed protection level is no longer exactly optimal; real systems re-run the calculation many times before departure (RM 101, chapter 04).
  • Demand for each fare is independent of what's on sale. In reality some high-fare customers buy the low fare when it's open and some low-fare customers trade up when it's closed. That buy-down is the subject of the buy-down simulator and chapter 05.
  • Low-fare demand is assumed large enough to buy every seat on sale to it. That doesn't change the rule: on a weak low-fare day the booking limit simply isn't reached and the spare seats stay open to high-fare customers. It does make the revenue figures here, and the gain over protecting nothing, too high for such days.
  • Normal demand, one flight leg, no cancellations, no-shows or overbooking (see the overbooking tab of the booking-limits simulator), and no connecting passengers (network bid prices).
