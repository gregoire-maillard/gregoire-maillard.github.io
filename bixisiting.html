Where should the next BIXI stations go?

If BIXI could add N stations tomorrow, which spots would put the most people, jobs and trips within a short walk of a bike?

Every hexagon on the map gets a demand score built from census population, jobs, distance to the metro and REM, and the trips already made at nearby stations. An optimization model then chooses the N new sites that cover the most demand that today has no station within 400 m. Change the number of stations and the weighting below, then scroll down to see where the returns fall off, who gains, and how the model would have done against the stations BIXI actually opened in 2025. Weights and radii are assumptions, not BIXI's criteria (see How it works).

    Airline parallel: opening stations is network planning

    Choosing new BIXI stations is the same question a network planner asks with an O&D demand map: where to add service so the network captures the most demand it doesn't reach today, knowing each addition costs the same and the best markets go first.

    How it works

    Study area and grid

    The study area is the island of Montréal (19 boroughs and 15 linked cities), Laval and the Longueuil agglomeration, where BIXI runs almost all its stations; a few stations in outlying towns and the separate Sherbrooke system are left out. It is cut into 9,345 hexagons of about 0.1 km² (Uber's H3 grid at resolution 9; centres roughly 300 m apart). Each hexagon centre is both a place where demand lives and a possible station site.

    Demand score assumption

    Four ingredients, each turned into a share of its study-area total so they can be added up, then weighted:

    Dh = 10,000 × ( wpop·poph/Σpop + wjobs·jobsh/Σjobs + wtr·Th/ΣT + wbixi·Bh/ΣB )
    Th = Σs ∈ metro, REM max(0, 1 − d(h, s) / 800 m)
    Bh = Σk ∈ BIXI stations tripsk · exp( −d(h, k)² / (2 · 300 m²) )
    • Population: 2021 Census, by dissemination area, spread over the hexagons by land area.
    • Jobs: 2021 Census count of workers by the census tract where they work. May 2021 was a pandemic month and people working from home aren't counted at a workplace, so downtown is undercounted.
    • Transit (T): a metro or REM station adds pull that fades to zero at 800 m, about a 10-minute walk. Bike-and-ride is one of the main uses of bike share.
    • Proven demand (B): starts plus ends at existing stations over a year, smeared onto nearby hexagons. It is the only ingredient that measures cycling directly, but it only exists where stations already are, so it favours filling gaps next to busy areas.

    The three presets weight these as: Balanced 40 / 20 / 15 / 25 (population / jobs / transit / trips), Residents first 70 / 10 / 20 / 0, Proven demand 15 / 15 / 10 / 60. Custom lets you set your own.

    Maximal covering location problem (MCLP)

    A hexagon is covered if its centre is within 400 m of a station (in practice: itself and its six neighbours). Demand that is already covered by today's network counts for nothing. Candidates are hexagons that are at least half land and whose centre is more than 300 m from every existing station, so the model never puts a station on top of another one.

    maximise   Σh ∈ U Dh · yh
    subject to   yh ≤ Σj ∈ C : d(h, j) ≤ 400 m xj    for every uncovered hexagon h ∈ U
                    Σj ∈ C xj = N
                    xj ∈ {0, 1} (open a station at j),   0 ≤ yh ≤ 1 (h is covered)

    It is solved exactly as a mixed-integer program with PuLP and the HiGHS solver (a few seconds per run, 0.1% optimality gap) for 10, 25, 50 and 100 stations and each preset, and for 0 to 150 stations in steps of 10 for the coverage curve. With Custom weights the page re-solves in your browser with a greedy heuristic (add the site that covers the most new demand, repeat); on the three presets the heuristic covers 98% or more of the extra demand the exact optimum covers, so the picture is the same.

    p-median, for comparison

    Maximal covering only cares whether demand is inside or outside 400 m. The p-median model instead minimises the demand-weighted walk to the nearest station, existing or new, so it also rewards moving people from 600 m to 350 m:

    minimise   Σh Dh · min( eh, minj open d(h, j) )     eh = distance to the nearest existing station, capped at 1 km
    ⇔ maximise   Σh Σj Dh · (eh − dhj) · zhj   s.t.   Σj zhj ≤ 1,   zhj ≤ xj,   Σj xj = N

    It is a much bigger program (about 160,000 assignment variables) built directly in HiGHS with a 90-second limit per run.

    Limitations

    • Demand is a proxy. Nobody counts bike-share demand where there is no bike share. Population, jobs and transit are what cities use to plan, and observed trips are biased toward places that already have stations.
    • No costs. Every station costs the same here. There is no open data on dock prices, electrical hookups for e-bike stations, rebalancing cost or each city's budget, and in practice the municipality that pays decides how many stations it gets.
    • No street-level feasibility. A hexagon centre is not a sidewalk. Real siting needs space, slope, visibility, snow clearing and parking removal, decided block by block.
    • Straight-line distances. 400 m as the crow flies; highways, rail yards and the river make some walks much longer.
    • Station size and network effects are ignored. A station is useful only if people can ride to another one; the model treats each site on its own and doesn't size docks.
    • Data vintages differ: 2021 census, 2024–2025 trips, a 2026 station list. The 2026 Census population counts come out in 2027.
