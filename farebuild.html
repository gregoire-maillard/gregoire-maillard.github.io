How a fare is built

From the fare an airline files to the price on the booking screen, one layer at a time.

An airline ticket price is a stack: one or more filed fares, each with its own code and rules, joined together by the rules of fare construction; then the airline's own surcharges; then taxes and charges set by the governments and airports at each end; and on top, the fare family and the extras the customer chooses. Step through a return trip in economy and watch the ticket fill up, then decode a fare basis and see how offer-based pricing changes the picture.

    Governments and airports set these, and the airline only collects them. Each one is defined by who pays it (departing, arriving or connecting passengers, by cabin or by distance) and shows on the ticket under a two-letter code. They don't depend on the fare, so a cheap ticket carries the same taxes as a dear one and taxes weigh most on the cheapest fares.

      Filed fares and offers

      what changes with offer-based pricing

      Everything above is the filed-fare model: prices published in advance and assembled by a pricing engine from fares and rules. Offer-based pricing, built on IATA's NDC and ONE Order standards, lets the airline answer each search with an offer it computes itself: a price that need not be one of a few filed steps, and a bundle it can put together for that request. The layers at the bottom of the stack stay: an offer still carries the same taxes and airport charges.

      How it works

      The model

      The price is built as a sum of layers, in the order a pricing engine applies them. For a ticket of fare components c sold in a country whose currency has the IATA rate of exchange ROE:

      Fare = round( ROE × Σc NUCc ),   with NUCc = Fc / ROE truncated to two decimals,

      where Fc is each component's filed fare in local currency: half the round-trip fare for each direction of a return. Fare construction is done in neutral units of construction (NUC) so components filed in different currencies can be added; the total goes back to the currency of the country where travel starts. Then

      Ticket = Fare + Σc YQ/YRc + Σt τt × BSRt,

      with YQ/YR the carrier-imposed surcharge per component and τt each tax or airport charge in its own currency, converted at a bankers' selling rate BSR. Fare families and extras are added on top: a higher fare family is a different filed fare (here the same fare with a different brand suffix), and seats and bags are separate items.

      In the example, both directions use round-trip fares halved and combined (a "half round trip" combination, allowed by each fare's category 10). The outbound departs in low season and the return in high season, so the two halves come from different fares: the ticket's two fare components. Each component's rules apply to its own direction, and the strictest rule of the two governs changes to the whole ticket. The fare's routing allows only the nonstop here; under the mileage system, a connecting routing is allowed up to the maximum permitted mileage (MPM, 120% of the ticketed point mileage), with surcharges in 5% steps up to 25% above it.

        Limitations

        • Fares, the carrier surcharge, the fare family step, seat prices and the two exchange rates (1 EUR = CA$1.60 and US$1.16, and an ROE of 1.38 for CAD) are illustrative. Real ROEs are published by IATA and change quarterly; bankers' rates change daily.
        • The French rates combine sources of different dates: the civil aviation rate is the one published for April 2025 to March 2026 and is indexed every April; the solidarity rate is the one in force since 1 March 2025; the Paris-CDG passenger charge is from Aéroports de Paris' first notification of 2026 tariffs. The security and safety rate at CDG is assumed at the top of the published range. French tax codes on tickets are left out because they vary by system.
        • Montréal–Paris is 5,524 km great-circle, just over the 5,500 km threshold for the long-haul solidarity rate; a few kilometres less would put it in the €15 band.
        • One passenger, an adult, nonstop both ways, no stopovers. Connections add taxes at each airport (often at reduced connecting rates), and itineraries with more points bring in fare construction checks this page skips: higher intermediate points, circle trips, open jaws and minimum fare checks.
        • US amounts are those in force from 1 October 2026 (customs and agriculture fees are indexed each October, the transportation tax each January). Canadian GST/HST doesn't apply to international air travel, but does to domestic and some transborder tickets.
        • Small items left out: a disability-assistance charge at Paris (about €2 to €5 per passenger depending on the airline), booking fees, and payment card surcharges.
        • Rules shown are a plausible set for a discounted economy fare, not any airline's filed rules.
