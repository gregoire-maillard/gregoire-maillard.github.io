Offer A/B test lab

Is the new offer better, or did you just get lucky?

Airline retailing teams test almost everything on the booking path: what goes in a bundle, what it costs, which fare family is shown first, whether a seat comes preselected. This lab plays one of those tests out on simulated shoppers. You design it, run it day by day, and read the result the way the team would: revenue per shopper first, with guardrails on bookings and complaints. Then you see the true effect you were trying to measure, and how often stopping early would have fooled you.

Illustrative numbers: the shoppers, conversion rates, booking values, complaint rates and test effects are invented assumptions and don't describe any airline.

1Design it before you startPick the smallest lift worth finding. That sets how many shoppers the test needs, so how long it runs. Set guardrails too: what B mustn't break.
2Run it to the endShoppers are split at random between A and B. Early numbers swing a lot; the interval narrows as shoppers pile up.

4 · The danger of peeking

Checking every day and stopping at the first significant result

The 95% in "95% confident" assumes you look once, at the end. Every extra look is another chance for noise to cross the line. On a dashboard that's checked daily, the line gets crossed by chance far more often than 5% of the time, and the test that crossed it gets shipped.

How it works

The shoppers

A shopper is a visit that reaches the step being tested, after a flight has been chosen. Each shopper is assigned to B with the probability you set, otherwise to A. In variant v a shopper books with probability p_v; a booking is worth R, the fare plus extras, drawn from a lognormal distribution with mean μ_v and coefficient of variation cv; each booking leads to a complaint (a customer-care contact or refund request about the offer) with probability c_v. B's true effect is three relative changes: on p, on μ and on c.

Revenue per shopper X = R if the shopper books, 0 otherwise Mean m_v = p_v × μ_v Variance σ_v² = p_v × μ_v² × (1 + cv²) − m_v² True lift (1 + Δp)(1 + Δμ) − 1

Daily traffic follows a weekly pattern with a little noise, and the test starts on a Monday. The number of shoppers in each arm and the number of bookings are binomial draws; every booking's value is drawn individually and complaints are Poisson. The random numbers for each day and each arm come from a fixed seed, so the same settings always give the same shoppers, and changing B's truth doesn't change A's shoppers. "New shoppers" moves to the next seed.

Designing the test

The primary metric is revenue per shopper, because it captures both sides of an offer change: more revenue from those who book, fewer people booking. With w_A and w_B the traffic shares, the minimum detectable effect δ (relative), confidence 1 − α and power 1 − β, the test needs

Shoppers in total n = (z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)² × σ_A² × (1/w_A + 1/w_B) / (δ × m_A)² Days n / shoppers per day, rounded up to whole weeks (7 to 56 days)

Rounding to whole weeks keeps every weekday equally represented. If the test would need more than eight weeks, it is capped and the page shows the smallest lift the capped test can detect.

Reading the result

Revenue per shopper uses a two-sample z-test with each arm's own variance (Welch, large-sample). The relative lift and its interval use the delta method. Conversion uses a two-proportion z-test; the intervals on conversion and complaints are on the ratio of rates (log scale).

Difference d = x̄_B − x̄_A, SE = √( s_B²/n_B + s_A²/n_A ), z = d / SE Relative lift L = d / x̄_A, SE_L = √( s_B²/(n_B x̄_A²) + x̄_B² s_A²/(n_A x̄_A⁴) ) Interval L ± z₁₋α/₂ × SE_L Rate ratio (conversion) log(p̂_B/p̂_A) ± z₁₋α/₂ × √( (1 − p̂_B)/k_B + (1 − p̂_A)/k_A ) k = bookings Rate ratio (complaints) log(ĉ_B/ĉ_A) ± z₁₋α/₂ × √( 1/C_B − 1/k_B + 1/C_A − 1/k_A ) C = complaints

Guardrails are non-inferiority checks. A guardrail is cleared when the interval rules out harm beyond the limit you set (conversion's lower bound above −limit, complaints' upper bound below +limit). It is breached when the harm is significant and the estimate is beyond the limit. Otherwise it is not cleared: the test can't rule out the harm. The verdict ships B only when the revenue lift is significantly positive and every guardrail is cleared.

Probability that B beats A uses a normal approximation to the posterior of d. With a flat prior it is Φ(d / SE). The sceptical prior is centred on no effect with a standard deviation of 2% of A's revenue per shopper, which reflects that most offer changes move revenue very little; it pulls the estimate towards zero: posterior mean d × τ²/(τ² + SE²), variance τ² SE²/(τ² + SE²). The expected loss of shipping B is E[max(0, −d)] under that posterior, per shopper.

Peeking and the sequential boundary

Looking at the z-score after every day and stopping at the first |z| ≥ z₁₋α/₂ is the peeking strategy. The sequential alternative is an O'Brien–Fleming boundary: at information fraction t_k (shoppers so far over shoppers planned), stop if |z_k| ≥ c / √t_k, with c chosen so that the overall false-alarm rate across all looks is α. The page finds c by simulating 20,000 Brownian paths at the actual look times (about 2.2 for 14 daily looks at 95%).

The false-winner demo runs 2,000 A/A tests (B identical to A) with your design. Each is simulated from daily totals, which by the central limit theorem behave like a Brownian motion in information time: z_k = W(t_k) / √t_k. That treats the variance as known, a negligible simplification at thousands of shoppers per day. A test counts as a false winner on the first look where it crosses the line, whichever arm it favours.

Limitations

  • B's effect is constant. Real offers have novelty effects (a new label gets clicks, then fades) and learning effects, so a short test can over- or under-state the long-run lift.
  • One shopper, one visit. Real shoppers come back several times before booking and may see both variants unless assignment is tied to a customer or a device, which also makes visits correlated.
  • Revenue per shopper is noisy because the fare dominates it. Real teams cut the variance with methods such as CUPED (adjusting for pre-test behaviour) or by measuring the offer's own revenue; this lab doesn't, so its tests are on the long side.
  • Revenue isn't profit. A preselected seat or a cheaper bag changes costs and displacement too, and a guardrail on complaints is only a proxy for the goodwill lost.
  • The normal approximations need enough bookings and complaints in each arm, which the shopper limits on this page ensure.
