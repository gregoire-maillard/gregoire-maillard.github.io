The next 50 km of bike lanes

If Montréal could add a fixed number of kilometres of protected bike lanes, which streets should get them to serve the most trips and stitch the existing network together?

Every BIXI trip of 2024 and 2025 is routed on the street network the way cyclists choose routes: protected lanes feel shorter, busy streets with no lane feel longer. An optimization model then picks the street stretches whose upgrade cuts that perceived effort the most, within a budget of kilometres. Move the budget, switch the objective, and compare the greedy rule of thumb with the exact model.

    What this means for the results

      Airline parallel: adding the routes that unlock connections

      A network planner doesn't judge a new route by its local traffic alone. A route earns its slot by the connecting O&D flows it unlocks: once Montréal–Lisbon exists, Toronto–Lisbon and Québec–Lisbon via Montréal become sellable too. A bike lane works the same way. A short stretch that joins two protected lanes is worth much more than its own length, because whole trips switch to the protected network once the gap is gone.

      Riders take the cheapest perceived routePassengers choose the best itinerary (a QSI or logit share model)

      Budget in kilometresFleet or aircraft-hours: a fixed number of new rotations to place
      Greedy pick vs exact MIPRanking routes one by one vs optimising the whole schedule (routes are complements, not independent)

      The greedy-vs-MIP gap above is the airline lesson in miniature: two half-built corridors are worth little, one finished corridor is worth a lot, and only a model that looks at combinations sees it. More in Network Planning 101 and OR 101.

      How it works

      The street network

      The street graph comes from OpenStreetMap: every street, lane, alley and path on the island of Montréal that a bike may use (motorways, private roads and plain sidewalks are left out). It has links between intersections. The City of Montréal's bike network file is matched onto it: a street link takes the City's facility when the City's line runs within 12 m of it and in the same direction (within 30°) over at least half its length.

      Class hereCity of Montréal types (TYPE_VOIE)km in the City file

      Where the City file says nothing, OpenStreetMap tags fill in (a mapped cycleway or cycle track counts as protected, a painted lane as painted). One-way streets are one-way for bikes too, unless the City or OpenStreetMap shows a contraflow lane.

      Route choice assumption

      Riders take the route with the lowest perceived cost, a common way to model cyclists' route choice:

      The design model

      minimise   ΣOD pairs k fk · Σp ∈ Pk ( cp zp − Σs ∈ p Δps wps )   −   β Σgaps g vg yg
      subject to   Σp ∈ Pk zp = 1   (each OD pair rides one route)
                      wps ≤ zp,   Σp ∈ Pk wps ≤ xs for every pair k and stretch s   (a route only gets the saving of stretches that are built)
                      yg ≤ xs for every stretch s in gap g   (the bonus needs the whole gap closed)
                      Σs kms xs ≤ B,    xs, yg ∈ {0, 1},   z, w ≥ 0
      • fk: BIXI trips a year between two hexagons; Pk: a pool of possible routes for that pair; cp: a route's perceived cost on today's network; Δps: what route p saves if stretch s becomes a protected lane (length × (penalty − 1); more on a one-way street, where the lane also allows riding both ways).
      • Route pool (column generation). The pool starts with each pair's cheapest route today, its cheapest route if every candidate were built, and two random part-built networks. Each time the greedy builds a network, the cheapest routes on it are added and the greedy runs again (two rounds).
      • Greedy. Repeatedly add the stretch, or the whole gap group, with the largest saving (plus bonus) per kilometre, until the budget is used up. It is fast and gives a full sequence from 0 to 100 km (the curves above), but it is myopic: a single block of a future corridor saves almost nothing alone, because riders only switch when most of the corridor is there.
      • MIP. The program above, solved with HiGHS over the OD pairs carrying 85% of trips and their route pools, warm-started from the greedy. The second constraint is the strong form of wps ≤ xs (it uses the fact that a pair rides only one route), which tightens the linear relaxation enough that each budget solves to optimality in seconds to minutes; any kilometres it leaves unused are then filled greedily. Only the effort objective has an exact model; with the protected-kilometres objective, riders' route choice and the planner's goal differ, which makes a two-level problem, so that objective uses the greedy only.
      • Protected-kilometres objective. Same route choice, but the greedy maximises trip-kilometres ridden on protected lanes instead of minimising perceived effort. It favours long continuous lanes on streets people already ride.
      • Gap bonus assumption
      • Exact check. Every solution shown on this page (tiles, list, table, MIP dots) is re-evaluated by routing all OD pairs again on the upgraded network, not just the route pool. The curves are the greedy's running estimate from the pool;

      Limitations

      • BIXI is a biased sample of cyclists. Bike-share trips are shorter, more central and more casual than trips on private bikes, and there is no BIXI where there are no stations, so the east and west ends of the island, and Laval and Longueuil (not modelled), carry little or no demand here. The counter check above shows how far off the picture can be. Better demand data (the ARTM origin-destination survey, Strava Metro) isn't open.
      • No cost per kilometre. A painted lane turned into a protected one, a lane on a 4-lane arterial and a lane needing a new bridge all count the same here. There is no open data on project costs, parking removal, snow clearing or utilities.
      • No political or physical feasibility. Street width, merchants, bus lanes, emergency routes and borough politics decide real projects. The model only says where the riding is.
      • Penalties are rough. They are fitted on about a thousand 2013–2015 GPS traces from a City app, on a network rebuilt from OpenStreetMap history. Riders' tastes, the share of e-bikes and the network have all changed since.
      • No new riders. Demand is held at 2024–2025 levels. A better network attracts new riders, so the gains here are a floor on what a plan would do for ridership, and the shifts in route are the only behaviour modelled.
      • Simplifications: no hills (Mont Royal matters), no traffic lights or turn costs, all-or-nothing routing (no route spread), hexagons of about 0.7 km² as origins and destinations, candidates limited to main streets (residential streets already rank as calm), and divided boulevards counted once.
