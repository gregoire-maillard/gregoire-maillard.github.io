Member lifetime value

A Gold member is worth more than a Base member this year, but the real question is what they are worth over the whole relationship: members move up and down the tiers, and some stop flying altogether. This calculator adds up the margin a member brings each year, follows where members of each tier go next, and discounts it all back to today.

Illustrative numbers: the programme, its tiers and every figure on this page are made up and don't describe any airline.

Scenario: keep more members in a tier

Value created across the member base

Suppose a retention effort (a softer landing, a targeted offer, better service recovery) cuts the share of one tier's members who lapse each year. The members it keeps behave like the tier's other members who stay: most remain, some move up or down. Members who start elsewhere also gain when they pass through that tier.

Scenario: more members take the co-brand card

Value created across the member base

Suppose a card campaign converts some members into cardholders. Each extra cardholder brings the bank's payment for the points they earn on the card, and costs the awards those points are eventually redeemed for. The model assumes the card changes nothing else: the member flies and lapses exactly as before.

How it works

Annual margin per tier

Every member of a tier is treated as the tier's average member. Their yearly margin is the contribution of their paid flying, plus what the bank pays for the points they earn on the co-brand card (for the share of the tier holding it), plus what partners pay for the points they earn with them, minus the cost of honouring the points they will redeem and the cost of their status benefits.

m(k) = F(k) + u(k) × C(k) × p_bank + Q(k) × p_partner − [ E(k) + u(k) × C(k) + Q(k) ] × ρ × c − B(k) F flight margin ($/year) E points earned from flying u share of members with the card C card points per cardholder Q points earned with partners B benefit cost ($/year) p price paid per point (bank, partners) ρ share of points eventually redeemed c cost of honouring a redeemed point

Redemption cost is booked in the year the points are earned, for the share expected to be redeemed: the timing of the awards themselves is ignored, which is close enough for a lifetime total. The breakage model follows that timing in detail. The default prices come from the illustrative example in Loyalty 101, chapter 07: the bank pays 1.2¢ a point and honouring one costs the airline about 0.7¢.

Tier migration as a Markov chain

The states are the five tiers plus Lapsed, a member who no longer flies or earns and brings no margin. P(i, j) is the probability that a member in state i this year is in state j next year. A member's position after t years is a probability over the states, found by applying the matrix once per year, starting from certainty in the starting tier s:

π₁ = e(s) (1 in the starting tier, 0 elsewhere) π(t+1) = π(t) × P

Retention of a tier is 1 − P(k, Lapsed): the share of its members still active a year later, whatever tier they land in. A lapsed member can come back through the Lapsed → Base cell.

Lifetime value

Lifetime value from starting tier s is the expected margin in each year, discounted at rate d and summed over a horizon of N years. Year 1 is the current year and counts in full, as in Loyalty 101, chapter 08.

CLV(s) = Σ_{t=1..N} π(t) · m / (1 + d)^(t−1) in matrix form, for every starting tier at once: CLV = Σ_{t=0..N−1} (P / (1+d))^t · m → (I − P/(1+d))⁻¹ · m as N → ∞

The member base value is Σ members(s) × CLV(s). The scenarios compute it twice, with and without the change, and report the difference. The tornado chart moves one lever at a time by the chosen percentage: retention through the lapse rate (a 20% lower lapse rate turns 7% into 5.6%), card uptake by its share, and flight margin, redemption cost and benefit cost by their amounts.

Where the default numbers come from

All the defaults are assumptions, chosen to look plausible for a large network airline and stored in assets/data/memberclv.json. The five-tier pyramid (5 million members, 90% of them Base) and the margins reflect the pattern described in Loyalty 101, chapter 06: a small share of members brings a large share of the money, and the top tiers cost the most to serve. The transition matrix has the usual shape: high tiers are sticky, most members who lose status drop one tier rather than to the bottom, and a quarter of Base members stop being active each year. The card economics follow chapter 07; for a sense of scale, Delta reported that American Express paid it $8.2 billion in 2025 under their co-brand agreement.

Limitations

  • Every input is an illustrative assumption, not an estimate for any real programme.
  • Averages per tier. Real members of the same tier differ a lot (a Gold member who flies twice a month versus one who just scraped in), and the value of an individual member needs a model of that member.
  • The chain has no memory and doesn't age: next year depends only on this year's tier, with the same odds every year. Real programmes have multi-year status, soft landings and lifetime tiers, and members' travel changes with their careers.
  • Flight margin is the whole contribution of the member's flying, not only the part the programme causes. This is the value of a member, not the value the programme creates: many of these trips would happen without it. Measuring that takes a holdout, as in chapter 09 and the promotion ROI calculator.
  • Card uptake is a share of each tier. In the card scenario the extra cardholders don't fly more or lapse less, which is conservative if the card builds loyalty and optimistic if people who take it would have been loyal anyway (chapter 07 discusses which way the arrow points). When one tier is targeted, a converted member holds the card while in that tier, not after moving.
  • The member base is closed: no new members join. The scenario values are before the cost of the retention effort or the card campaign; compare them with the break-even amounts shown.
